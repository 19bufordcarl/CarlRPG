# Exploration

## Dungeon Exploration

An expedition to a dungeon should feel like a foray into a dangerous location, one where a single misstep can spell disaster. Make too much noise and monsters could come pouring from every direction.


#### Time and Order of Play

In dungeon exploration, time passes in 10-minute spans. The PCs act in the order they wish. NPCs act after the PCs.


#### Movement

The party can move up to 60 feet, or 120 feet if they use no actions or activities.


#### Actions and Activities

As part of moving, you can use an action.

#### Doors

One never knows what might be on the other side of a door in a dungeon.

Normal Doors. These are as thick and heavy as a door found in a house. They can be opened or closed as a free action.

Heavy Doors. Heavy doors are difficult to move. They require a Strength check to open and swing close unless spiked open.

Secret Doors. Secret doors blend into a wall or are otherwise concealed. They require an Intelligence

(Investigation) check to discover.


#### End of Spans

At the end of each span, the dungeon might specify that you check for special events or adjust the location of a monster or trap.


### The Threat Timer

While the characters explore a dungeon, the DM sets a timer to ring every 10 minutes in real time. The timer determines when the DM checks to see if the dungeon’s threat menaces the party.


#### The Threat Roll

Each time the alarm sounds, the DM rolls a d6. If the roll is a 1, the characters face a threat. If the roll is a 6, the characters gain a boon. Otherwise, the DM sets that die aside. The next time the alarm rings, the DM rolls each

die they set aside and an additional d6.

For example, the DM rolls 1d6 for threat the first time the alarm sounds. The DM rolls a 4, so they set aside the die. The second time the alarm rings they roll a d6 and the one die they set aside. They roll two 3s, so they set both dice aside. The third time, the DM rolls a d6 and the two dice that they set aside. They roll two 1s and a 4. They set aside one die and use the two dice that rolled 1 to determine the nature of the threat.

If the characters leave the area covered by the threat and don’t return for at least one day, the process starts over at one die when they return. Pause the timer while the group takes a break. Stop tracking it when they leave the dungeon.

## Crafting a Dungeon

To create a dungeon, follow these steps.


### Draw a Map

Use a sheet of graph paper to draw your dungeon map. Treat each square as covering an area 10 feet on each side. Use half the sheet for the map. Save the other half for notes on locations and areas in the dungeon.

Draw rooms on the map by creating squares and rectangles on the graph paper. Leave at least two squares between each room. Rooms should be at last three squares on each side.

For each room, roll 1d4 to determine how many exits it has. Place the exits randomly. Half the exits should be doors, the others are open doorways.

Connect the room exits. To other rooms or link them up into intersections and other crossroads. Add a passage that ends in a stairway leading up to the surface. If you want to add more levels below, add a passage that ends in a stairway going down.

Finally, for each room with only one entrance roll a d6. On a 1 or 2, the door or doorway leading into it is instead a secret door.


### Stock the Map

For each room, roll on the table below.

D12 Result 1 – 2 Monster without treasure 3 – 4 Monster with treasure 5 Unguarded treasure 6 Secret 7 – 12 Empty


#### Monster Without Treasure

Roll on the encounter table for the dungeon’s level to determine the monster.


#### Monster With Treasure

Roll on the appropriate encounter and treasure tables for the dungeon level.


#### Unguarded Treasure

Roll on the level’s treasure table. There is an equal chance that the treasure is hidden, that it is hidden and

trapped, or that it is obvious but trapped.


#### Secret

The room holds a secret about the dungeon. Create one!


#### Empty

There’s nothing here. However, a blank room can be boring. Look at the nearby rooms that have monsters, treasure, or secrets. Pick one and add some detail here that hints of what lies ahead. A monster might leave signs of its passage, a mural might depict the treasure, and so on.

## Outdoor Exploration

Outdoor exploration is resolved in hour long travel shifts. It follows this sequence of play:

1. Navigation 2. Activities 3. Roll for random encounters


### Navigation

The party picks the direction they want to travel or names a specific destination they want to move toward.

The terrain may require a Tracking check to see if the party stays on track. If the characters fail the roll, they accidentally travel in the wrong direction for the hour. They veer one compass point off their path, chosen at random. For example, if the group wishes to travel north

and fails to navigate, they instead travel northwest or northeast. They travel off course for 1 hour before realizing their error.

Terrain. The general nature of the terrain the group travels through. Clear includes roads, deserts, tundra, plains, and grasslands. Forest is woods, swamps, and low hills. Mountains covers high peaks and other daunting terrain.

Check Time. How often to make a Tracking check. If the characters travel less than this time, they don’t need to check. They also don’t need to check while following a road or path.

Travel Rate. The miles per hour the group moves.

### Activities

The characters can interact with the environment as they travel. While the players can improvise anything with the DM’s approval, here are the most common actions.


#### Forage

Once per day, the party can attempt to forage. Each character who wishes to forage makes a Wisdom check. On a success, they find enough food and water to last the day for 1d3 people.


#### Navigation

The character who navigates cannot use any other activity while traveling.


#### Scout

A character can attempt to scout ahead for danger. The scout makes a Move Silently check. On a successful roll, the characters surprise any

monsters they encounter during the hour of travel.


#### Search

A character can search as they travel. Make a Hear Noise check. On a success, they spot any hidden details, landmarks, or other details they might otherwise overlook.


### Food and Water

If you do not have food and water for two days, make a Constitution save. If you fail, you take a failed death save and cannot lose death saves until you eat and drink.


### Hazards

Some areas of wilderness have hazards that pose a threat to the party. Note on the map the chance that the characters might encounter it.


### Obstacles

A river, cliffs, or other obstacles can block the party’s progress. Usually, the party needs to make a group roll to cross them. The obstacle specifies what happens if the group fails.


### Roll for Random Encounters

At the end of each hour, roll a d6. On a 1, a random encounter occurs.


#### Monster Encounters

If the characters encounter hostile creatures as part of a random encounter, check for surprise. The monsters appear 100 + 2d20 feet away from the party.


#### Monster Reaction

The DM can judge a monster’s reaction based on the situation and the details on that monster’s personality,

role in the dungeon, and so on. Otherwise, when in doubt the DM can have the party roll a group Charisma check. If the party succeeds, the monster is neutral toward them. If they fail, it is hostile.


### Resting

The characters can travel for up to 12 hours each day. If random encounters are possible, roll for encounters each hour as described above.

## Hazards

Dungeons and other dangerous areas are filled with hazardous elements. Here are a few of the more common ones.


### Falling Damage

A character falling into a pit or chasm takes 1d6 bludgeoning damage per 10 feet fallen and lands prone.


### Environmental Hazards

Fire, smoke, lava, acid, and other threats can prove deadlier than a monster. The table below summarizes how much damage a hazard inflicts. The damage type should reflect the nature of the hazard.

Some hazards inflict damage and cause failed death saves, reflecting the overwhelming pain and shock they cause.

You can take the damage from a hazard once per round. Once you take it, you can’t take it again until your next turn.

Severity Damage Death Saves Minor 1d6 0 Moderate 3d6 0 High 6d6 1 Lethal 12d6 2


#### Hazard Examples

Minor hazards include everyday dangers, like a campfire, a flask of acid, smoke from a moderate fire, or a pot of boiling water. These are dangerous but rarely life threatening.

Moderate hazards are larger scale and can kill or severely injure the typical person. They include a bonfire, a pool of acid, smoke from a burning building, or immersion in boiling water.

High severity hazards kill the average person. They include

brief exposure to lava, poison gases, and full immersion in acid.

Lethal hazards kill even the mightiest adventurers and include immersion in lava and similar dangers.

## Vision and Light

Lighting and obscurement both play a role in determining how far you can see.


### Lighting

Here are the lighting levels, from darkest to brightest.

Darkness: You can’t see into darkness and creatures can hide in areas of darkness.

Dim Light: You can see in dim light, but the shadows allow some creatures to hide here.

Bright Light: You can see in bright light, and there is too much light to hide.

Sunlight: Sunlight is a special case. Some creatures suffer drawbacks when they are in sunlight. Sunlight can be bright or dim. In either case, it triggers effects that depend on sunlight.

Overlap: The brightest source of light determines the lighting in an area.


### Sources of Light

Each source of light typically sheds dim light in a broader radius.

Source Dim Radius Torch 30 feet Lantern 40 feet Candle 10 feet


### Obscurement

Fog, steam, smoke, and foliage can hinder vision despite the lighting conditions. Obscurement reduces the distance you can see but does not block attacks or spells.

Lightly Obscured: If an area is lightly obscured, you can only see things within 30 feet.

Heavily Obscured: If an area is heavily obscured, you can only see things that are within 5 feet.


### Senses

Unless otherwise noted, a creature has access to the five standard senses of smell, taste, touch, sight, hearing. A creature might also have access to a supernatural sense listed below. Each of these senses includes a range, the distance that they can reach.

Blindsight. You ignore darkness and obscurement in your area. You treat creatures and objects within 30 feet as if you could see them in dim light. Objects, such as walls, block blindsight.

Darkvision. Darkvision allows you to see in the dark, though with some limitations. You treat darkness within 30 feet as dim light. Darkvision does not function while you are in an area of bright light or sunlight. Obscurement blocks it as normal.

## Objects

Objects can be damaged by attacks and by some spells. The DM may allow a creature to break it automatically with an attack or utilize action.


### Damaging Objects

An object has an AC and hit points.


#### Armor Class

Objects larger than a size Medium creature do not have an AC. Attacks against then automatically hit. Otherwise, an object’s size determines its AC. If you try to attack an object held by a creature, use the creature’s AC.

## Traps

Traps present a constant, lurking threat.


### XP

Traps have an XP value that reflects their overall strength.


### Triggering a Trap

A trap describes what causes it to trigger.

Targets. Traps usually target an area or a specific creature that triggered them.

Death Saves. Traps are deadly because they catch targets unaware and unleash lethal attacks. Traps can inflict damage, and some especially deadly ones grant failed death saves.
